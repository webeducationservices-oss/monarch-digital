Your restaurant profit margin is the share of every sales dollar you keep after paying all the bills. In most of the industry it's a thin number: published ranges for full-service restaurants commonly sit around 3 to 5 percent net. Where you land depends on your format, your rent, and how tightly you run food and labor. Here's how to calculate your margin, typical ranges by format, what the 30/30/30 rule means, and the levers that move the number most.

## How to calculate restaurant profit margin

Three formulas do most of the work.

- **Gross profit margin** = (sales minus cost of goods sold) ÷ sales
- **Prime cost percentage** = (cost of goods sold + total labor) ÷ sales
- **Net profit margin** = net profit ÷ sales

Cost of goods sold is your food and beverage cost. Total labor includes wages, payroll taxes, and benefits for everyone, managers included. Net profit is what's left after those two and everything else: rent, utilities, marketing, card fees, repairs, insurance, and the rest.

Here's a worked example with round numbers for a full-service restaurant.

| Line | Amount | Share of sales |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | $1,000,000 | 100% |
| Cost of goods sold (food and beverage) | $300,000 | 30% |
| Total labor | $330,000 | 33% |
| Prime cost | $630,000 | 63% |
| Occupancy (rent, property taxes, insurance) | $90,000 | 9% |
| Other operating expenses | $230,000 | 23% |
| Net profit before income taxes | $50,000 | 5% |

- Gross profit margin: ($1,000,000 minus $300,000) ÷ $1,000,000 = 70 percent
- Prime cost: $630,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 63 percent
- Net profit margin: $50,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 5 percent

The gross margin looks healthy at 70 percent, and that's the trap. Labor, rent, and operating costs take almost all of it.

## Typical restaurant profit margins by format

These are commonly published industry ranges for net profit margin. Sources vary, so treat them as rough benchmarks and compare yourself with restaurants of a similar format and market.

| Format | Commonly published net margin | Why it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Full service | 3 to 5% | More staff per guest and more space per seat |
| Quick service and fast casual | 6 to 9% | Lower labor per sales dollar and faster turns |
| Food trucks | 6 to 9% | Low occupancy costs and a short menu |
| Catering | 7 to 8% | Production planned around confirmed orders |
| Bars with tight cost control | 10 to 15% | Lower cost of goods on drinks |

Fine dining is a full-service format, so it's usually measured against the same range. Higher check averages help, but the costs climb with them: more staff per table, pricier ingredients, wine inventory, and more square feet per guest.

## Is a 10 percent profit margin good for a restaurant?

By published industry ranges, yes. Ten percent net sits above the typical full-service range and at the top of the quick-service range. If you're there, find out which lines are carrying you so you can protect them as costs change. If you're well below it, prime cost is usually the first place to look, because food and labor are the two biggest lines on the P&L.

## What is the 30/30/30 rule for restaurants?

The 30/30/30 rule is a rule of thumb for splitting sales into rough thirds. About 30 percent goes to food and beverage cost, about 30 percent goes to labor, and the remaining third covers everything else: rent, utilities, marketing, repairs, and profit. A common version adds a fourth number, 30/30/30/10, which reads as 30 percent food, 30 percent labor, 30 percent overhead, and 10 percent profit.

On $1,000,000 in sales, that's $300,000 for food, $300,000 for labor, $300,000 for overhead, and $100,000 left over as profit.

It's a useful gut check. Its limits are real, though.

- **The profit line is optimistic.** Few full-service restaurants keep 10 percent. The published ranges above sit well below that for most formats.
- **Formats don't fit the same thirds.** A bar's cost of goods runs lower. Fine dining labor runs higher. Quick service labor runs lower.
- **Rent varies by market.** A prime downtown lease can push occupancy well past what the rule assumes.
- **Food and labor trade off.** Scratch cooking can lower food cost and raise labor, while buying prepared products does the reverse. That's why prime cost, food and labor together, is the better target. Many operators aim for a prime cost around 60 percent of sales.

Use the rule to spot a line that's far out of range, then set your real targets from your own P&L.

## Five levers that move your margin

1. **Food cost.** On $1,000,000 in sales, every point of food cost is $10,000. Drop from 30 to 29 percent and, with everything else equal, net margin goes from 5 to 6 percent. Start with accurate counts and recipe costing: [how to calculate food cost](/blog/how-to-calculate-food-cost/).
2. **Labor scheduling.** Build schedules from your sales forecast, and cut early on slow nights. A point of labor is worth the same $10,000. For more on the biggest two lines, see [prime cost and labor cost](/blog/prime-cost-labor-cost/).
3. **Menu engineering.** Price and place dishes by contribution margin, the dollars each plate leaves after food cost, and give your best sellers the best spots on the menu.
4. **Check average.** Train servers on suggestive selling, especially beverages, starters, and desserts. An extra $2 per cover on 40,000 covers a year is $80,000 in sales.
5. **Waste, comps, and voids.** Review them every night. Small leaks add up fast over a month.

Lever four in numbers: $80,000 in added sales at a 70 percent gross margin is $56,000 in gross profit before any added labor. Not all of it reaches the bottom line, but it shows why server training belongs in a profit conversation.

## How to find your own margin

Most owners have the data already. Here's the routine:

- **Close the books monthly**, with inventory counted at the same time each month so food cost is accurate.
- **Use a standard chart of accounts** so every month, and every location, is categorized the same way.
- **Track prime cost weekly.** Waiting for the monthly P&L means a bad week becomes a bad month.
- **Compare against your own history** first, then against published ranges for your format.
- **Read a trailing 12 months** alongside each month. A slow January and a packed December both distort the picture on their own.

## Profit, cash flow, and EBITDA

A profitable restaurant can still run short of cash. Net profit on the P&L doesn't include loan principal payments, new equipment, or owner draws, and all three take real money out of the bank. Watch cash flow alongside margin, and build a reserve before the slow season arrives.

Multi-unit groups and lenders often talk about EBITDA, which means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Restaurant-level EBITDA, sometimes called four-wall EBITDA, measures each location before corporate overhead, so you can compare one restaurant with another. A location with a strong four-wall number can still lose money once you add the cost of the support team above it, so track both.
