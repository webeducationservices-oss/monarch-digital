Multi-Location SEO for Restaurants: Get Found at Every Address

Monarch Digital August 4, 2026 6 min read

Multi-location SEO decides which of your restaurants shows up when someone nearby searches for dinner, brunch, or your name plus a neighborhood. It's common for a group's original location to rank well while the newer ones barely show up. Each address needs its own Google profile, its own page on your website, and its own reviews, all managed to one standard. Below is how to set up each piece, who should own it, and how to measure results location by location.

Multi-Location SEO Starts With One Profile per Address

Every location needs its own verified Google Business Profile. For a lot of guests, that map listing is the first and only thing they see before they call or book.

Set each one up the same way:

  • Name. Use the name on your sign. Google's guidelines don't allow extra keywords in a business name, and a stuffed name can get a listing suspended.
  • Category. Use the same primary category at every location of the same concept.
  • Hours. Set regular and holiday hours for each location, and update them before every holiday.
  • Links. Point each profile's website link to that location's page on your site, and add the right menu and reservation links.
  • Photos. Post real photos of that dining room, that patio, and that team.
  • Access. Keep ownership at the company level and give each GM manager access to their own location.

If you run 10 or more locations of the same business, Google offers bulk verification, which saves verifying each one separately. Our guide to Google Business Profile for restaurants covers setup in more detail.

Give Every Location Its Own Page

Each address should have its own page on your website, and each Google profile should link to it. A single locations page with five addresses gives search engines and guests very little to work with.

Element What to include Why it matters
URL A clean path like /locations/downtown/ One clear page per address
Name, address, phone Exactly as they appear on the Google profile Matching details across the web
Hours Regular and holiday hours Fewer wasted trips and fewer angry calls
Menu That location's menu The right dishes and prices
Reservations and ordering Links for that location Turns a visit into a booking
Local details Parking, patio, private dining, what's nearby Matches what locals search for
Photos That dining room and team Shows guests what to expect
Map An embedded map Directions in one tap
Structured data Restaurant schema with that address and hours Helps search engines read the page

Write different copy for each page. If you copy one page five times and swap the address, none of them gives Google much reason to rank it. Write about what's specific to that spot: the neighborhood, the patio, the private room, the chef running that kitchen.

For structured data, use the Restaurant type from schema.org on each location page, filled in with that location's address, phone, hours, cuisine, and menu link. Your web developer can add it once as a template, and each page pulls its own details. Test every page with Google's free Rich Results Test after it goes live.

Give Each Location a Local Voice

Multi-location marketing works best when the brand looks the same everywhere and each location still sounds like part of its neighborhood. Decide up front which pieces belong to the brand and which belong to the location.

Keep these consistent at every address:

  • Your brand name, logo, and photo style
  • How you describe the core menu and the food
  • The tone of your review replies and social posts

Let each location own these:

  • Photos of its own dining room, team, and specials
  • Local events, from trivia nights to the first weekend of patio season
  • Partnerships with nearby businesses, schools, and charities
  • Google posts about what's happening at that address this week

Google posts appear on that location's profile, so a post about Thursday's live music reaches the people already looking at that exact restaurant. A GM who sends one good photo and two lines a week gives your marketing lead plenty to work with.

Keep Listings Consistent Everywhere

Guests also find you on Apple Maps, Bing, review sites, and delivery apps. Each location's name, address, phone, and hours should match everywhere they appear.

Once a quarter, search each location's phone number and address. You'll find duplicates, old listings from a previous tenant, and maybe the location that moved two years ago and still shows its old address somewhere. Merge or remove duplicates, and when a location moves, changes hours, or closes, update every listing the same week.

Handle Reviews Location by Location

Reviews belong to each profile, so treat them that way. Reply at every location, ideally in the GM's voice and within a day or two. AI can draft replies to save time, and a person should approve every reply before it posts.

Ask for reviews the right way. Google's policies don't allow incentives for reviews, so skip the free dessert in exchange for five stars. A simple ask at the end of a good visit, or a follow-up email with a direct link, keeps you within the rules.

Reviews also show you how each location runs. When one location's reviews keep mentioning slow weekend service, that's an operations problem showing up in your marketing, and the fix starts in the kitchen and on the floor.

Give Every Task an Owner

Treat local SEO like any other standard you run across locations, with a written task list and a named owner for each task, the same way your restaurant SOPs work.

Task Owner How often
Update regular and holiday hours Marketing lead, confirmed by each GM Before every holiday or change
Add new photos Each GM Monthly
Reply to reviews Each GM, from approved drafts Within two days
Publish a Google post Marketing lead Weekly, and for every event
Audit listings for duplicates Marketing lead Quarterly
Review the scorecard Owner or operating partner Monthly

Measure Each Location Separately

Add tracking tags, called UTM parameters, to each profile's website link so your analytics shows visits by location. Then build a monthly scorecard for each address: calls, direction requests, website clicks, reservations, new reviews, and average rating. Compare locations side by side. A location with strong search visibility and weak reservations has a different problem than one nobody can find.

Each profile's performance view in Google Business Profile also shows the searches people used to find that location. Read them monthly. If guests keep finding your uptown spot by searching for brunch or a private dining room, those words belong on that location's page and in its next few posts.

If nobody on your team has time for this, it's exactly the kind of monthly work a restaurant SEO partner should handle and report on.

If Your Locations Have Separate Websites

Groups that grew one location at a time often end up with several old websites. Moving them onto one site with a page per location makes everything easier to manage, as long as every old URL redirects to its new home so you keep the links you've earned. When we moved a private dining club in Vail, Colorado off its old website platform, we set up 37 redirects, and its SEO audit score went from 66 to 98.
