What to Put in a Restaurant Employee Handbook

Monarch Digital September 1, 2026 6 min read

A restaurant employee handbook tells your team how things work at your place: how they get paid, how scheduling works, what you expect on the floor and in the kitchen, and what happens when something goes wrong. A clear one saves managers from answering the same questions every week, and it gives you a written record when a dispute comes up. Below is what to include, section by section, and how to roll it out so people read it.

Have an employment attorney review it. Employment law varies by state, and sometimes by city. Treat everything below as an outline, and have an employment attorney licensed in your state review your policies before you hand out the handbook and every time you update it.

What Goes in a Restaurant Employee Handbook

Section What to include Ask your attorney about
Welcome Your story, what you stand for, what great service looks like here Wording that could read like a contract
Employment basics Equal opportunity, anti-harassment, how to report a problem, employment status At-will language and required state notices
Hiring and onboarding Paperwork, the training plan, any introductory period How you describe an introductory period
Scheduling and attendance Availability, when schedules post, swaps, call-outs, no-call no-shows Local predictive scheduling rules
Pay and timekeeping Pay periods, clocking in and out, breaks, overtime approval Your state's meal and rest break rules
Tips Tip pool or tip-out rules, credit card tips, tip credit notice if you use one Federal and state tip rules
Dress code Uniform, shoes, hair, jewelry, who provides what Uniform cost rules and grooming protections
Food safety Handwashing, illness reporting, allergen procedures Your health code's reporting rules
Alcohol service ID checks, refusing service, staff drinking Your state's liquor laws
Safety Kitchen safety, reporting injuries Workers' compensation notices
Conduct Phones, social media, staff meals, comps, guest interactions Rules that touch employee speech
Time off PTO, sick leave, holidays, jury duty, family leave State and city paid sick leave laws
Leaving Resignation notice, final pay, returning uniforms Final paycheck timing in your state
Acknowledgment A signature page that records which version was signed How long to keep signed copies

Keep the welcome section short and in your own voice. It's the one part of the handbook a new hire reads for the feel of the place, so let it sound like you talking at pre-shift.

Sections That Need Extra Care

Tips

Write the exact rules: who's in the pool, how shares or percentages work, when tips get paid, and how credit card tips are handled. Under federal law, managers and supervisors can't keep any part of employees' tips, and if you take a tip credit, you have to tell tipped employees about it in advance. State rules can be stricter. Vague tip rules start arguments that end up on a manager's desk.

Scheduling and call-outs

Say when schedules post, how to request time off, how swaps get approved, and exactly how to call out, including who to contact and how much notice you need. Define a no-call no-show and what happens after one. If you operate in a city or state with predictive scheduling rules, your policy has to match them.

Pay, breaks, and overtime

Explain the pay schedule, how to clock in and out, and what to do about a missed punch. Require approval for overtime, and pay for every hour worked whether or not it was approved. You can coach someone who skips the approval step, and the hours still get paid. Federal law doesn't require meal or rest breaks, and some states do, so your break policy should follow your state's rules.

Food safety and illness

The FDA Food Code expects food employees to report certain symptoms, like vomiting, diarrhea, or jaundice, to the person in charge, and your local health code may add more. Spell out who to call and when, and back it up with a sick policy that doesn't punish people for following it. Pair it with your allergen procedure so every server and cook follows the same steps.

Phones, social media, and conduct

Keep phones off the floor during service, and don't let staff post photos of guests without permission. Be careful with confidentiality and social media rules, though. Federal labor law protects employees' right to talk about pay and working conditions, so a blanket ban on discussing wages can get you in trouble. Run this section past your attorney.

Dress code

Tie grooming rules to safety and neatness, and apply them to everyone in a role. Plan for religious dress, which federal law generally requires you to accommodate, and natural hairstyles, which many states protect under CROWN Act laws. Our post on server dress code covers uniform ideas and what a policy should say.

Staff meals, shift drinks, and discounts

Write down who gets a staff meal and when, what the employee discount covers, and whether off-duty staff can drink at the bar. If you offer a shift drink, check your state's liquor rules first. Clear rules here keep comps from turning into a gray area that a new manager has to sort out alone.

What to Leave Out

A handbook covers policy. Keep these out of it:

  • Recipes and procedures. Those belong in your SOPs and training, where they can change without reissuing the handbook.
  • Promises you can't keep. Skip phrases like guaranteed raises or permanent employment.
  • Every possible offense. List the big ones and say that other conduct can lead to discipline too.
  • Old policies. If you've stopped doing something, take it out so the handbook matches how you run today.

How to Roll It Out

A handbook only works if people read it and you can show they received it.

  • Keep it readable. Plain words, short sections, a table of contents, and a Spanish version if part of your team reads Spanish first.
  • Walk through it on day one. Spend 20 minutes on the sections that come up most on a shift: tips, call-outs, allergens, and phones.
  • Collect e-signatures that record the version. When the handbook changes, you need to know who signed which version. At a private dining club in Vail, Colorado, the training academy we built records which version of the handbook each person signed.
  • Quiz the key policies. A few questions on tips, call-outs, and allergens show you who read it.
  • Date every version. When something changes, publish the new version, note what changed, and collect new signatures.
  • Review it every year. Laws change, and so does your restaurant. Put a yearly review with your attorney on the calendar.

This is where the handbook and your restaurant training program should meet. When the handbook lives inside your training platform, the signature, the quiz, and the version history sit in one place, and a new hire reads each policy right before the lesson that depends on it. Policies set the rules, and your restaurant SOPs show how the work gets done, so link the two.
