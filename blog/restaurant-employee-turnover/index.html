Restaurant Employee Turnover: What It Costs and How to Cut It

Monarch Digital August 18, 2026 6 min read

Restaurant employee turnover is a cost most owners feel every week and rarely put on paper. Someone gives notice on a Thursday, a manager scrambles to cover Friday, and the new hire who starts next week needs training while everyone else works short. The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks quits by industry every month, and accommodation and food services regularly posts one of the highest quit rates of any industry. Plenty of what pushes people out the door is in your hands, though: the first two weeks, the schedule, the tip rules, and whether anyone notices good work.

How to Measure Restaurant Employee Turnover

Turnover rate is a simple formula:

Turnover rate = separations during the period ÷ average number of employees during the period × 100

Say you started the year with 38 employees and ended with 42, so your average headcount is 40. If 24 people left during the year, your turnover is 24 ÷ 40 = 0.6, or 60 percent. For a more accurate average, add up your headcount at the end of each month and divide by 12.

Run it monthly too. A monthly rate uses that month's separations and that month's average headcount, and it shows a problem forming long before the year-end number does. A spike right after a menu change, a new manager, or the end of patio season tells you where to look.

Then break the number down, because the overall rate hides the patterns that matter.

  • By role. Compare dish, prep, line, servers, and bartenders. The roles that churn fastest are where to look first.
  • By tenure. Count how many people leave in their first 90 days. Early exits are the clearest signal about your hiring and your first two weeks.
  • By location and manager. If one store or one manager loses people much faster than the rest, you've found your first fix.

What Each Departure Costs

Most of the cost of a departure hides in hours, which is why it rarely gets its own line on the P&L. The table below is an example for a single line cook. The numbers are illustrations, so swap in your own wages and hours.

Cost What to count Example
Recruiting Job posts, a referral bonus, hours screening applicants $300
Hiring Manager hours for interviews, references, and paperwork $200
Onboarding Uniform, new-hire kit, payroll setup $150
Training Trainee wages during training, plus the trainer's time $1,200
Slower output Slower tickets, remakes, and waste in the first weeks $500
Coverage Overtime for the crew while the spot is open $400
Total $2,750

Run the same math for one front-of-house role too, since training time and pay differ. At 24 departures a year, the line cook example alone adds up to $66,000. Even if your real number comes in at half that, it's money you'd rather put into raises, training, or the patio you keep putting off.

Why People Leave Restaurants

When you ask people why they left, the answers tend to land in a few familiar places.

  • A rough first week. No plan, no trainer, and a packed Saturday on day three.
  • Unpredictable schedules. Posted late, changed often, with uneven sections.
  • Murky pay and tips. Nobody can explain the tip-out, or pay trails what the place down the street offers.
  • Nobody notices. Good work goes unrecognized, and feedback only shows up when something goes wrong.
  • Nowhere to go. No visible path from busser to server, or from prep cook to the line.
  • A manager problem. One manager who plays favorites or yells on the line can empty a station.

How to Cut Restaurant Employee Turnover

Hire with a clear picture of the job

Some early exits trace back to the interview, when the job sounded different from the one the person got. Be specific about the hours, the pace, how tips work, and what a Saturday feels like. If you use a working interview or a stage, pay for the time, keep it short, and pair the candidate with someone who models your standard. A clear picture up front gives people one less reason to walk out in week two.

Fix the first two weeks

Have the uniform ready, the schedule set, and a trainer assigned before day one. Then give every new hire a structured plan with a daily lesson, floor time with a trainer, and a sign-off before they move on. Our server training checklist shows what that looks like over ten days. A real restaurant training program tells a new hire you planned for them, and people notice.

Check in at set points through day 90

Put short check-ins on the calendar so they happen even on a slammed week. Ten minutes is enough.

When Who What to ask
End of week one Trainer and manager What's confusing so far? Do you have everything you need?
Day 30 Manager What's harder than you expected? Who's been helpful?
Day 60 Manager What would you change about your shifts or sections?
Day 90 GM What role do you want next, and what would get you there?

Write down what you hear and follow up on it before the next check-in.

Post schedules early and keep them steady

Publish the schedule at least a week ahead, and two weeks if you can. Honor availability, rotate the good sections fairly, and keep changes to a minimum. When something does change, tell only the people it affects. For a private dining club in Vail, Colorado, we built scheduling that texts each person their week when it's published and texts only the affected people when something changes later. Our restaurant schedule template walks through building a week from your sales forecast.

Make pay and tip rules plain

Write down exactly how tips are pooled or shared, when they're paid, and how credit card tips are handled, then walk every new hire through it. Know what restaurants near you pay for each role. If you can't match it, be clear about what else you offer, like steady hours, staff meals, or a path to a better role.

Build a ladder people can see

Show each role what the next one requires. A busser who knows what it takes to become a server, or a prep cook who can see the path to the line, has a reason to stay. Role-based training makes the ladder visible, and cross-training gives you coverage when someone does leave.

Recognize good work out loud

Read guest compliments at pre-shift and name the server. Thank the dishwasher who stayed late on a slammed night. Recognition costs nothing, and it's one of the easiest things to forget when the week gets busy.

Hold stay interviews

Exit interviews tell you why people left. Stay interviews tell you why people stay and what might push them out. Once a quarter, sit down with each key person for ten minutes and ask what they'd change. Then fix one thing they mention, and tell them you did.

Track every exit

Keep a simple log of each departure: role, tenure, location, manager, and the reason given. Review it every quarter next to your turnover rate, and the patterns will show up fast.
