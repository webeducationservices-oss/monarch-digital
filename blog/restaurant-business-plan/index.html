How to Write a Restaurant Business Plan (With an Outline)

Monarch Digital November 4, 2025 7 min read

A restaurant business plan turns your concept into numbers a lender, landlord, or investor can say yes to. It's also your own reality check, because it puts every assumption about covers, check average, rent, and labor on paper before you sign a lease. The plans that get funded tend to be short, specific, and built on math a stranger can follow. Below is an outline that works, what each section needs to answer, and the two calculations every reader checks first: the sales forecast and break-even.

Who reads your plan and what they look for

Your plan will land on several desks, and each reader skims for something different.

  • Lenders want to know how the loan gets repaid, how much of your own money is going in, and whether the team has run a restaurant before.
  • Landlords want confidence you'll pay rent for the full term, so they read your experience, your concept, and your build-out budget.
  • Investors and partners want to know how much profit the restaurant can make, how soon, and what happens if sales come in light.

Be concrete for all of them. A line like "about 2,000 office workers within a five-minute walk and no sit-down lunch spot on the block" does more work than a page of adjectives.

A restaurant business plan outline

Use these sections in this order. If you're applying for an SBA-backed loan, the SBA's own guide follows a similar structure. Write the executive summary last, once the numbers are settled.

Section What it needs to answer
Executive summary The concept, the location, how much money you need, and why it will work, on one page
Concept Service style, cuisine, hours, seat count, average check, and ownership
Market analysis Who lives, works, and eats in your trade area, and when
Competition Your nearest competitors, their prices, and the gap you'll fill
Location and lease The site, square footage, rent, lease term, and build-out required
Menu and pricing A sample menu, your target food cost, and how your prices compare
Management and staffing Who runs what, their experience, your staffing plan, and how you'll train the team
Marketing Your pre-opening, launch, and ongoing plans, each with a budget
Operations How service runs, your key suppliers, your technology, and your systems
Financials Startup costs, sources and uses of funds, a sales forecast, projected P&L and cash flow, and break-even
Funding request How much you need, what it pays for, and the terms you want
Appendix Resumes, the full menu, a floor plan, quotes, and letters of intent

Concept, market, and competition

Describe the concept in a sentence a banker could repeat: a 70-seat neighborhood restaurant with a full bar, dinner six nights a week, and brunch on weekends. Then walk your trade area at the hours you plan to open. Note which competitors have a wait on a Tuesday, what their entrees cost, and what guests complain about in their reviews. Those complaints are your opening.

Management, staffing, and training

Readers bet on people. List each owner and manager with their restaurant experience. Then show a staffing plan by role and shift, plus a training plan for opening: who trains whom, for how many days, and how you'll hold standards once the doors are open. A team that's trained before the soft opening makes every number in your forecast more believable, so give restaurant staff training a real place on the opening timeline.

Operations

Describe how a night runs: reservations and walk-ins, the line, expo, side work, and closing. Name the technology you'll use for the POS, reservations, and scheduling. This is the section where an experienced restaurant operations consultant can pressure-test your assumptions before a lender does.

How to build the sales forecast

Build sales from expected demand, one daypart at a time. The core formula is simple.

Annual sales = covers per service × average check × services per year

Say you're planning that 70-seat restaurant, open for dinner six nights a week. You expect 100 covers on an average night with a $40 average check, food and beverage combined.

  • 100 covers × $40 = $4,000 a night
  • $4,000 × 6 nights = $24,000 a week
  • $24,000 × 50 weeks = $1,200,000 a year, leaving two weeks for holidays and closures

That's about 1.4 turns a night on 70 seats (100 ÷ 70), which a reader can sanity-check quickly. Back it up with comparable restaurants nearby, and model a slower first few months instead of assuming you'll hit full volume on opening night. The SBA's guide asks for a five-year outlook, with quarterly or even monthly projections for the first year.

Break-even: the number every reader checks

Break-even is the sales level where profit hits zero. To find it, split your costs in two. Fixed costs stay the same whether you're busy or slow. Variable costs rise and fall with sales.

Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio

The contribution margin ratio is the share of each sales dollar left after variable costs, or 1 minus (variable costs ÷ sales).

Back to the example. Monthly fixed costs (rent, insurance, salaried managers, loan payments, and base utilities) come to $35,000. Variable costs (food and beverage cost, hourly labor, card fees, and supplies) run 65 percent of sales, so the contribution margin ratio is 35 percent.

  • Break-even sales = $35,000 ÷ 0.35 = $100,000 a month
  • At a $40 check, that's 2,500 covers a month
  • At 25 dinner services a month (300 a year), that's 100 covers a night

The forecast and break-even are the same number. On paper, this restaurant never makes a dollar. Better to find that now than six months after you sign the lease.

Original plan Revised plan
Monthly fixed costs $35,000 $28,000
Variable costs, share of sales 65% 65%
Contribution margin ratio 35% 35%
Break-even sales per month $100,000 $80,000
Break-even covers per night 100 80
Monthly profit at forecast sales $0 $7,000

In the revised plan, a lower rent and a leaner salaried team cut fixed costs by $7,000 a month. Break-even drops to 80 covers a night, and at the forecast 100 covers the restaurant earns about $7,000 a month before taxes ($100,000 × 0.35 minus $28,000), roughly 7 percent of sales. Raising the average check or adding weekend brunch are other ways to widen the gap.

One caution: this math treats hourly labor as fully variable, which is a simplification. You'll always need a minimum crew on a slow night, so keep your assumptions conservative.

Startup costs and sources of funds

Your financials also need a startup budget and a sources and uses table that balances to the dollar. Uses are everything you'll spend before you open: build-out, kitchen equipment, furniture and fixtures, deposits, licenses, opening inventory, pre-opening payroll and training, marketing, and working capital to carry you through the ramp. Sources are where the money comes from, such as your own equity, loans, and investors. For every line item and how to budget it, see how much it costs to open a restaurant.

Mistakes that weaken a plan

  • Forecasting from capacity. Assuming every seat turns twice every night.
  • No ramp and no cushion. Skipping the slow first months or the working capital line.
  • Targets with nothing behind them. If the plan says 30 percent food cost, the sample menu should show it.
  • Rent that eats the margin. Many operators try to keep occupancy costs under about 10 percent of sales.
  • Vague marketing. A marketing section with no budget, calendar, or owner.
  • Claiming there's no competition. Every restaurant competes with something, even if it's the kitchen at home.

Check that your projected margins fit your format, too. A full-service plan showing 15 percent net profit will raise eyebrows, so compare yours to typical restaurant profit margins by format.
