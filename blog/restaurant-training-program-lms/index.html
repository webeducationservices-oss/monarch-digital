Restaurant Training Program: How to Build It, When to Add an LMS

Monarch Digital June 9, 2026 8 min read

A good restaurant training program gets every new hire from first shift to floor-ready on a predictable schedule, and it teaches the same standard no matter which trainer is on that day. Programs tend to break in the same places. Training lives in one great server's head, the binder is two menus out of date, and nobody can say for sure who passed what. This guide covers how to build a program that holds up, and how to tell when a custom LMS, short for learning management system, is worth building.

What every restaurant training program needs

  • Role-based tracks. Each position gets its own academy, with its own lessons and sign-offs. A host and a line cook need different curriculums.
  • A day-by-day plan. Every day has a clear goal, so the trainee, the trainer, and the manager all know what the new hire should be able to do by the end of it.
  • Daily quizzes. Short checks tied to that day's lessons.
  • Trainer sign-offs. A qualified trainer watches the skill performed and signs for it.
  • Handbook e-signatures. Each hire signs the current handbook, with a record of which version and when.
  • Menu training. Every dish, its ingredients, its allergens, and how to describe it.
  • Bilingual content. Lessons and quizzes in the languages your team works in.
  • Records. Who completed what, when, what they scored, and who signed off.

Build it by role

Role Core skills Example sign-off
Server Steps of service, menu and allergens, POS, wine basics Takes a full table from greet to check with the trainer watching
Host Reservations, wait quotes, seating rotation, phones Runs the stand for a shift with the trainer nearby
Bartender Recipes, pour standards, responsible alcohol service Passes a pour test
Back waiter Table numbers, running food, resets, side work Runs a section's food for a full service
Line cook Station recipes, plating, ticket times, food safety Works the station through a service at standard
Prep Prep lists, knife work, labeling and dating, storage Completes a full prep list to spec
Dish and porter Machine operation, chemical safety, closing duties Closes the dish station alone
Leads and managers Opening and closing, cash, cuts, guest recovery Runs a shift with a manager shadowing

Write each role's content from what your best people actually do. Shadow them, write down the steps, and have them check the draft. That's how you keep the standard that made them your best people in the first place.

Build it by day

A day-by-day structure gives every role the same rhythm. Here's a 10-day framework you can adapt:

Day Focus Check before the next day unlocks
1 Welcome, culture, handbook, safety, and a walk of the building Handbook signed, safety quiz
2 Role basics and standards Quiz
3 Menu, part one Menu quiz
4 Menu, part two, plus allergens Menu and allergen quiz
5 Shadow a trainer on a live shift Trainer notes
6 Shadow again, taking on small pieces of the job Quiz and trainer notes
7 Reverse shadow, with the trainee working and the trainer watching Skill sign-offs
8 Reverse shadow on a busier shift Skill sign-offs
9 A full shift with light support Trainer sign-off
10 Final quiz and manager check Cleared for solo shifts

The rule that makes it work is simple. The next day unlocks only when the trainee passes the quiz and the trainer signs off. That keeps a new hire from drifting onto the floor half-trained because the schedule got tight. For a detailed front of house version, see our server training checklist.

Quizzes and sign-offs: proof of learning

Quizzes prove someone learned the material. Sign-offs prove they can do the job. You need both.

Keep quizzes short, five to ten questions, and pull every question from your own menu and SOPs:

  • Which dishes on tonight's menu contain tree nuts?
  • What's the build and garnish on the house old fashioned?
  • How long can cut greens hold in the walk-in under our labeling standard?
  • What do you tell a guest who asks about the fish of the day?

Set a passing score, such as 80 percent, and let trainees retake a quiz after reviewing the lesson. Track misses by question. If half your new hires miss the same allergen question, fix the lesson.

Sign-offs need a written standard so every trainer judges the same way. A pour test for bartenders, a timed station check for line cooks, and a full table for servers are all easy to define and easy to observe.

Handbook e-signatures

Every hire should sign the current employee handbook before their first shift, and your records should show which version they signed and when. When you change a policy, publish a new version and collect signatures on the update. That record matters the day you need to show someone acknowledged a policy. Our guide to the restaurant employee handbook covers what to put in it.

Menu training that keeps up with the menu

Menu training goes stale the moment the menu changes, so build it from the kitchen's source of truth. Each dish needs:

  • A short description servers can say out loud
  • Key ingredients and preparation
  • Allergens, pulled from the kitchen's allergen matrix
  • A photo of the plated dish
  • Pairings and upsell notes where they fit

When the kitchen adds a dish or swaps an ingredient, the lesson and its quiz questions should update the same week. Seasonal menus get a fresh round of tastings and quizzes at every change.

Training in Spanish and other languages

If part of your team learns best in Spanish, every lesson and quiz should switch languages with one tap, and the two versions should match line for line. Have a bilingual manager or a trusted cook review the translations, since a literal translation can miss how your crew actually talks on the line. Keep dish names exactly as they appear on the menu so guests and staff use the same words.

Trainers, practice, and life after day 10

Train the trainers. Pick trainers for consistency and patience, and walk them through the sign-off standard before they judge anyone. Give them a lighter section on training shifts so they can actually watch, then spot-check their sign-offs.

Practice before the floor. Let new hosts and servers rehearse in your POS training mode, if it has one, or on a practice copy of your booking tools filled with fake guests, before they touch a real check or reservation.

Keep it going. Push a short lesson and quiz whenever the menu changes, collect new handbook signatures when a policy changes, and use the same academies to cross-train people for promotions.

Off-the-shelf training software vs. a custom LMS

Factor Off-the-shelf training software Custom LMS
Content Course libraries, plus whatever you upload Written from your recipes, SOPs, and handbook
Setup time Fast Weeks to build and review
Roles and progression Basic course assignments Role academies, day-by-day locks, trainer sign-offs
Menu training Manual uploads Built from your dish file and updated with the menu
Languages Varies by product Every lesson in the languages your team uses
Logins Usually a separate system Can share one login with your operations tools
Cost Usually a per-user subscription A build cost plus ongoing support

When off-the-shelf is enough

  • One location with a small, steady team
  • A simple menu that rarely changes
  • Mostly compliance training, like harassment prevention or food safety basics
  • No need for trainer sign-offs or detailed records

When a custom LMS pays off

  • You hire constantly across many roles
  • Several locations need one standard
  • Your service style is specific, like fine dining, a private club, or hotel food and beverage
  • The menu changes often and carries a lot of allergens
  • You need records of who passed what and who signed which handbook version
  • Your team needs bilingual training
  • You want one login per hire across training and operations, with access cut the day someone leaves

Those are the operations our restaurant staff training work is built for. Either way, an LMS doesn't replace state-required certifications, like food handler cards or responsible alcohol service training from approved providers. It should track them, with expiration dates and reminders.

How to measure whether training works

Metric What it tells you
Days from hire to first solo shift Whether the plan runs on schedule
Quiz scores by lesson Which lessons need rewriting
Sign-off completion Whether trainers are following through
New hire retention at 30, 60, and 90 days Whether people stay after training
Comps and voids by new staff Where skill gaps cost money
Ticket times for new cooks Whether kitchen training sticks
